If you do custom work, the most common message you get is a version of the same thing:

*"How much for a sleeve?"*
*"What do you charge to tint a truck?"*
*"How much is a groom for a doodle?"*
*"Ballpark on a bathroom remodel?"*

None of those can be answered honestly without more information, and all of them arrive while your hands are busy. So what should an AI receptionist do with them?

## The rule we build to: it quotes what you've published, and nothing else

If you have a posted price — a $35 haircut, a $150 diagnostic fee, a first-visit exam, a shop minimum — the receptionist can and should give that number instantly. That's a fact about your business, it's already public, and making someone wait for it is a good way to lose them.

Everything else, it captures rather than quotes.

That line matters more than it sounds, because **a wrong quote is worse than no quote.** Two ways it burns you:

- **Too low, and you're anchored.** Someone was told $200 on the phone. You see the piece, it's a $600 job. Now you're either eating the difference or having an argument that starts with "but your receptionist said."
- **Too high, and they're gone.** They never called back, you never knew they existed, and you have no idea what it cost you.

A missed call, you can recover with a text an hour later. A bad number you can't take back.

## What "capture instead of quote" actually looks like

Capturing well is a real skill, and it's most of the value. The receptionist's job is to arrive at your callback already knowing what you'd have to ask anyway.

For a **tattoo**, that's the idea, rough size, placement, colour or black-and-grey, references, and roughly when they want it. An artist coming out of a four-hour session to *"upper arm, about palm-sized, black-and-grey, here's a reference, flexible on timing"* is in a completely different position than one facing *"how much for a tattoo?"*

For a **groom**, it's breed, size, coat condition and how matted they are — the four things that decide both the price and how long the table is occupied.

For **window tint or PPF**, it's the vehicle, how many windows, and which film.

For a **trade**, it's what's wrong, where the property is, how urgent it is, and whether they own the place.

Same shape every time: the variables that move the number, gathered before the number gets mentioned.

## The phrase that does the work

The single most useful sentence an AI front desk can say is some version of:

> *"I can get all the details over to [you] and they'll confirm the price — I don't want to guess and be wrong."*

Customers accept that immediately, because it's obviously true and obviously in their interest. Nobody wants a made-up number either. What they don't accept is silence, or a voicemail, or a form that goes nowhere.

You keep the pricing decision. They get a fast, human-sounding answer. Nothing is promised that you have to walk back.

## Where this applies more than people expect

It isn't just custom trades. The same discipline covers:

- **Insurance questions.** A practice can say which plans it's in-network with. What a specific person's plan actually covers is a question for a human, because a wrong answer there becomes a billing dispute rather than a lost lead.
- **Timelines.** "How long will it take?" is a quote in disguise. Published service durations, fine. A promised completion date on work nobody has looked at yet, no.
- **Feasibility.** Whether a cover-up will work, whether a colour will lift, whether a repair is worth doing on a 200,000-mile car. Those are professional judgments, and a front desk that offers one is speaking for you without your permission.

## The test

Here's the way we think about it when we set one of these up:

> **Could a brand-new receptionist on their first day answer this from the notes you left them?**

If yes, the AI should answer it, instantly, at any hour. If no, it should capture the details and get it to you fast.

That's not a limitation of the technology. It's the same boundary you'd give a human at your front desk — the difference is this one never gets tired at 6 PM and decides to wing it.
