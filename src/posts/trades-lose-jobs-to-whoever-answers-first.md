A homeowner's furnace quits on the first genuinely cold night of the year. Here's what they actually do.

They search. They call the first number. It rings out, so they call the second. That one goes to voicemail, and they don't leave one — because when you're cold you don't wait for a callback, you call the third number.

Somebody picks up. That's the job.

Not the best reviews. Not the nicest van. Not the company that's been in the valley for thirty years. Whoever answered.

## The cruelty of it is that this scales with success

If you're bad at your job you have a quiet phone and plenty of time to answer it.

If you're good, you're on a roof, in a crawlspace, or elbow-deep in a panel. You are unreachable precisely because you're in demand — and the calls you're missing are arriving at the rate your reputation earned.

Growth makes this worse, not better. That's the part that catches people out. Hiring a second tech doubles the work you can do and doubles the hours nobody is near a phone.

## The calls that matter most arrive when nobody's in the office

Ordinary business calls are easy. Somebody wants a quote on a water heater at 2 PM on a Tuesday, and if they get voicemail they'll probably call back.

The valuable calls don't behave like that:

- **The furnace that dies at midnight in January.** Emergency rate, and usually a bigger conversation about replacement afterwards.
- **The pipe that lets go on a Sunday.** Every minute it runs is more damage, so nobody is waiting until Monday.
- **The whole valley calling at once after a hailstorm.** Fifty roofing enquiries in a morning, and your crews are on a roof.
- **The no-cool call in the first July heat wave.** Same day or they find someone else.

Every one of those arrives outside office hours or during a surge. That's not a coincidence — it's the same weather that created the emergency.

## Answering isn't the same as booking

Here's where a lot of automation goes wrong for trades. The instinct is "let it book the job." That's the wrong shape.

You can't dispatch a truck without knowing what's wrong, where it is and how bad it is. And you certainly can't bid a job the customer hasn't shown anyone. What actually helps is screening:

- **What's the problem, in their words.**
- **Where's the property** — is it even in your service area.
- **How urgent** — this decides everything downstream.
- **Own or rent** — because a renter often can't authorise the work, and finding that out on site is an expensive way to learn it.

Now you're looking at a list of qualified jobs with enough detail to decide what's worth a truck roll tonight and what waits until Thursday. That's a different thing from a calendar full of appointments somebody's software booked on your behalf.

And it never bids. Trades pricing depends on what you find when you get there. A front desk that guesses a number is creating an argument for you to have later — [we've written about that here](/post/can-an-ai-receptionist-quote-a-price/).

## The emergency line

One thing that has to be right before any of this goes live: some calls to a trade aren't service calls at all.

Somebody smells gas. A carbon monoxide alarm is going off. There's smoke, or a panel is arcing. Those callers need to be told to get out and call 911 — not booked, not captured as a lead, not sold anything.

That instruction has to sit above every other rule the system has, and it has to be calibrated so that *no heat at ten below* is treated as urgent-but-normal rather than as a 911 emergency. Get that backwards in either direction and you've got a system nobody trusts. [The longer version is here](/post/what-should-an-ai-receptionist-do-in-an-emergency/).

## What the honest version looks like

For a trade, a front desk that works looks like this:

- **Missed calls become texts within seconds** — so the caller who couldn't reach you is in a conversation instead of your voicemail.
- **The routine questions get answered any hour** — service area, licensed and insured, whether you do that kind of work, rough scheduling windows.
- **Real jobs get screened and land on your calendar as estimates**, not in a queue you work through at 8 PM.
- **Genuine emergencies reach a human fast**, and unsafe situations get told to call 911 first.
- **Everything needing a tradesman's answer comes to you** with a summary of the conversation.

None of that replaces you. It buys back the specific hours when being unreachable is costing the most.

## Heating season is the deadline

If you do heating work, this is worth sorting out **before** the season rather than during it. The first cold snap is when the phone goes from steady to relentless, and it's a bad moment to be setting up new systems.

The homeowners calling that week aren't loyal to anyone yet. They're cold, and they're working down a list.
